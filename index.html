Artificial Intelligence: Helpful or Harmful?

A simple guide to what AI is, how we use it, and why we should be careful.

So, what exactly is AI?

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a way of making computers do tasks that normally involve human thinking, like recognizing images, understanding language, or making predictions. Not all AI works the same way. Some programs follow rules, while others learn patterns from large amounts of information.

For example, when a chatbot answers a question, it uses patterns learned during training to generate a response. This can be useful, but it does not mean the chatbot understands everything or always tells the truth. The OECD explains AI in more detail.

Why do people use AI?

I think one of AI's biggest advantages is that it can save time. Students can use it to explain difficult topics or make practice questions. At work, it can help organize information, draft messages and look for patterns in data.

AI can also make technology easier to use. Automatic captions, translation and tools that describe images are helpful for people with different accessibility needs. But these tools work best when people check the results instead of assuming they're perfect.

A small example from studying

Imagine struggling with a maths concept. You could ask an AI tutor to explain it in simpler words and give you a new practice problem. That's helpful. But copying its answer without understanding the steps would defeat the purpose of learning.

What could go wrong?

AI can make mistakes and still sound very confident. It may also repeat biases from its training data or create privacy problems if people share sensitive information with it. Another serious concern is the rise of deepfakes: fake voices, photos or videos that can seem real.

Real example: a deepfake scam

In 2024, reports described how an employee at the engineering company Arup was tricked during a video meeting featuring fake versions of colleagues. Scammers persuaded the employee to transfer about HK$200 million. The case shows why seeing a familiar face on a screen is not enough to prove someone is genuine. Read the report.

My takeaway: important requests, especially about money, should be confirmed through a different and trusted method.

What might happen in the future?

I expect AI to become part of more everyday services, from education to healthcare and transport. That could be exciting, but it also means a mistake or outage might affect more people.

AI needs computers, data centres and electricity. The International Energy Agency expects data-centre electricity demand to rise substantially by 2030, although its projections include more than just AI. If electricity networks do not expand fast enough, growing demand could create local problems. That doesn't mean AI will automatically cause blackouts. See the IEA's research.

My prediction is that people will rely on AI more, but human supervision will become more important, not less. Schools, companies and governments will need to think carefully about security, responsibility and what happens when systems fail.

How this website was made

I chose to make this website simple because my goal was to explain AI in a way that anyone could understand, not to create a complicated design. I divided the topic into a few sections: an introduction, benefits, risks, a real example and a look at the future. I used short paragraphs and headings so readers could follow the ideas easily.

The site uses HTML for the writing, headings and links, and CSS for the background colour, spacing and text. I avoided complicated animations and graphics. The menu at the top links to different parts of the same page. I also used a flexible page width so the text is easier to read on phones and computers.
